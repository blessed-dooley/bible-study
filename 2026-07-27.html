Selfless Love

The Essence of Christian Life and Witness Lesson 5, All to the Glory of God Part 3 of 7
July 27, 2026
✦ ☩ ✦
📌 TL;DR

Before the harbor wakes, Paul is already at the workbench, cutting leather to fund a message he could have preached for pay. Today he reads out his rights like a lawyer. Meals, a wife's travel, a working wage, each one legitimate, one of them commanded by the Lord Himself. Then comes the strangest word a man with a resume like his ever wrote. Nevertheless. He leaves the whole list on the table so no one can say the gospel was ever about his paycheck. A right laid down for love preaches louder than a right enforced.

🔁 Connection to Yesterday

Yesterday ended with a trophy nobody should want, the strong winning their meat debate while a weak brother tore inside. Paul told the confident crowd to surrender the trophy, and it would be fair to ask whether he has ever done that himself. Today he answers by opening his own ledger. It turns out the man asking Corinth to set down a steak has been setting down his salary for years. The argument moves from their kitchen to his workshop, and the principle gets a face. Knowledge asks what I am allowed to do. Love asks what I am willing to lose. Paul shows us his losses on purpose.

🗺️ Historical & Cultural Context
The Hand That Paid You Owned Your Voice

In Paul's world, a public teacher had two respectable funding models, and both came with a leash. He could charge fees the way the traveling professionals did, which meant shaping the product to keep customers happy. Or he could land a wealthy patron, move into the great house, eat at the great table, and become part of the household's glory. That second arrangement had a price everyone understood. The philosopher on retainer flattered his host, laughed at his jokes, trimmed his lectures to fit the room. Money in that culture was never just money. It was a thread, and the person holding the other end could pull. Corinth, a city built by freedmen who had clawed their way up the patronage ladder, knew exactly how the game worked.

Which is why Paul's third option scandalized people. The apostle to the Gentiles rented bench space in a leather shop and made tents with his own scarred hands (Acts 18:3). Manual labor was slave's work in the eyes of Corinth's upwardly mobile, an embarrassment in an apostle's resume. Paul wore the embarrassment like a uniform. No fee meant no customer to please. No patron meant no strings on the message. When he stood up to preach the crucified Messiah, every listener in the room knew one thing for certain. Nobody was paying him to say it.

📖 Into the Word

The light comes up over the workbench before it reaches the harbor, and the apostle is already cutting leather. He has seen the risen Lord. He has planted this very church. And he is doing slave's work for bench rent, on purpose. In today's passage Paul finally explains the strangest line item in his ministry (1 Corinthians 9:1-6). He is not defensive about his rights. He lists them, proves them, and then shows Corinth his open hand, because the confident crowd from yesterday needed to see what laying down a right actually looks like when it costs something.

1 Corinthians 9:4-6
☩ Open in Bible App
4Have we no right to food and to drink? 5Have we no right to take along a believing wife, as do the other apostles and the Lord’s brothers and Cephas? 6Or are Barnabas and I the only apostles who must work for a living?

Three questions, three entitlements. The word Paul keeps using is exousia, Greek for a right or authority, something genuinely owed to you, and he is not being theoretical. The right to eat and drink (1 Corinthians 9:4) meant financial support, a minister living from the ministry. The right to a believing wife (1 Corinthians 9:5) meant the church funding a ministry couple's travel, as it did for others. The right to refrain from working (1 Corinthians 9:6) meant not needing a second job to survive. Every other apostle drew on these. Paul establishes that the rights are real precisely because he is about to waive them, and a waiver only means something when the claim is valid.

Each of those rights had faces attached. Priscilla and Aquila traveled as a married ministry team (Romans 16:3), and so did Andronicus and Junia (Romans 16:7). Paul happened to be unmarried (1 Corinthians 7:8), but the door was open, and the church would have carried them both. As for working, his partner Barnabas was the man who once sold a field and laid the money at the apostles' feet (Acts 4:36-37), generous to the bone and still earning his own bread. These were not men who could not collect. They were men who chose not to.

1 Corinthians 9:14
☩ Open in Bible App
In the same way, the Lord has prescribed that those who preach the gospel should receive their living from the gospel.

Paul argues the point like a lawyer stacking exhibits. Soldiers eat from the army's budget, vineyard workers eat the grapes, the ox treading grain gets to eat the grain, and if ministers sow spiritual seed, reaping material support is only fair (1 Corinthians 9:7-11). If other teachers in Corinth drew support, Paul had a stronger claim than any of them (1 Corinthians 9:11-12). Then he closes the case with the highest authority there is. The Lord Himself commanded it, the same principle Scripture states as the worker being worthy of his wages (1 Timothy 5:18). Supporting gospel workers is not a loophole or a favor. It is Jesus' own design. Hold that firmly, because it makes what Paul does next astonishing.

1 Corinthians 9:12
☩ Open in Bible App
If others have this right to your support, shouldn’t we have it all the more? But we did not exercise this right. Instead, we put up with anything rather than hinder the gospel of Christ.

Nevertheless. The entire chapter pivots on that one word. Paul wins the argument and then declines the winnings, and he tells us exactly why. In Corinth, taking the money would have tied strings to the message, made him one more hired voice in a city full of them, given every cynic a reason to plug their ears. So he absorbs the cost in his own body, the early mornings, the calloused hands, the lean weeks, and offers himself as a living example of self-denial for the sake of others (1 Corinthians 9:1-18). The goal was never martyrdom for its own sake. He became all things to all people to win as many as possible, so that they may be saved (1 Corinthians 9:19-23). The gospel was free. Paul made sure it looked free.

🎯 Street Level
The Salesman Who Talked You Out of It

You walk into the phone store the way everyone does, braced. The man who greets you works on commission. You know it, he knows you know it, and every word he says has to climb over that fact to reach you. Then something strange happens. He looks at the model in your hand, the one with the price tag that would make his week, and tells you the cheaper one suits you better. Bigger battery, same camera, four hundred dollars less. He says it plainly and waits. You stand there doing the math on what that sentence just cost him, and the whole room changes. Suddenly you believe his answer about the warranty. You believe him about the case. You will come back to this man for a decade and send your mother to him by name, all because he left his own money on the table in front of you.

Paul understood exactly what that salesman understands. He spends most of the chapter proving he had every right to be paid, soldiers eat from the budget, the ox eats the grain, the Lord Himself commanded it, and then he leaves the whole claim uncollected. In Corinth, every traveling teacher had a fee, and every listener had learned to hear messages as pitches. One more hired voice would have changed nothing. An apostle cutting leather for bench rent could not be explained away, because nobody could name what he was getting out of it. That is still the move love makes. Waive the favor owed, the credit deserved, the fee you could have charged, and something happens to the words you say next. They stop sounding like a pitch. The people around us have heard a lifetime of messages with strings attached, and they check for the strings first. Nobody wonders what the unpaid man is selling.

🌍 Today's World, Ancient Truth
The Giver Who Kept No Strings

Since 2019, MacKenzie Scott has given away more than 19 billion dollars to over 2,000 nonprofits, and the strangest part is not the amount. It is what she refuses to collect. There is no application to fill out. Her team researches organizations quietly, and the first contact is usually the news that the money is coming. No naming rights, no seat on the board, no required reports, no restrictions on how to spend it. Every string a donor at that scale is entitled to hold, she drops. The philanthropy world predicted disaster, dependency, waste, a financial cliff when the gifts ran out. So the Center for Effective Philanthropy spent three years checking, surveying 813 nonprofit leaders and combing the tax filings of more than a thousand recipient organizations. Nearly ninety percent said the gift strengthened their long-term stability. Recipients held roughly twice the operating reserves of similar organizations. The feared misuse never materialized.

Here is the part Paul would recognize from his workbench. The gifts work because of what she declined. A donation with strings keeps the donor at the center, and everyone involved quietly performs for the hand that funds them, which is exactly how patronage ran Corinth. Scott removed herself so completely that the organizations became the story, and the researchers found their leaders standing taller, staying longer, burning out less, trusted people rising to the trust. Paul cut leather for the same reason, so that no one could ever ask what the preacher was getting out of it. The principle has not aged a day. When someone with a valid claim leaves it uncollected, the people watching stop hunting for the angle and start believing the message. Love that pays its own way is the most convincing argument most people will ever see.

✝️ The Christ Connection

Paul's list of unclaimed rights is a photocopy, and the original hangs in Bethlehem, Nazareth, and Jerusalem. Though He was rich, for your sake He became poor, so that by His poverty you might become rich (2 Corinthians 8:9). Jesus had the right to be worshiped without interruption, and He took a feeding trough. He had the right to a home, and foxes ended up better housed than He was (Matthew 8:20). In the garden He named the right most of us would have used, twelve legions of angels a single word away (Matthew 26:53), and He left the word unsaid. The Son of Man did not come to be served but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45). Every unclaimed right of Jesus became a claimed sinner. That is the arithmetic of the cross, and Paul was just doing the same math with a smaller number.

✅ Personal Application Challenge
Today, leave one legitimate claim uncollected, on purpose, for a named person's good. Pick something genuinely owed to you. The credit for work someone else is getting praised for. Your turn to choose. The apology you are still waiting on before you warm up. The twenty dollars a friend forgot. The last word you could easily win. Choose one, decide before God that you will not collect it this week, and do not announce the sacrifice to anyone. Watch what uncollected debts do to a relationship when love is the one absorbing the cost.
🙏 Closing Prayer

Father, we came to You today holding our list of rights, and we found Jesus holding His, unclaimed. We confess that we are quick to collect. Quick to defend our time, our credit, our comfort, our turn, quick to invoice the people around us for everything they owe us. Thank You for the Son who had every right to stay rich and chose our poverty instead, who could have called the angels and stretched out His hands for the nails. Teach us His arithmetic. Show each of us the one claim You are asking us to leave on the table this week, and give us the quiet strength to leave it there without applause. Let the people near us find the gospel easier to believe because we made it look free. In Jesus' name, Amen.
