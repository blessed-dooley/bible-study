Repentance and Forgiveness

Growing in a Relationship with God Lesson 10, Repentance and Forgiveness Part 1 of 7
May 30, 2026
✦ ☩ ✦
📌 TL;DR

Israel walks through a parted sea on a Wednesday and bows to a metal calf by the next month, and the same chapter that records the smashed tablets records the God whose tablets they smashed standing on the mountain proclaiming His own name as "merciful and gracious."

  • ☩The Memory Text comes from a fisherman at the end of a long life writing one short sentence that holds both halves of how rescue actually works. If we confess. He forgives.
  • ☩Israel at the foot of Sinai, Moses with broken tablets in his hand, a calf still smoking on the altar, and the God they had just betrayed announcing His own name to the man they sent up to ask if there was any way back.
  • ☩Repentance and forgiveness are not two separate doctrines. They are the two sides of one healing, the human side and the divine side, and the cross is the place they finally meet without anyone getting hurt twice.
  • ☩By Thursday a king has thrown the wedding banquet, the doors are open to the highways and the back alleys, and the only question that matters is whether we are wearing the robe the Host already provided at His own expense.
🔁 Connection to Last Week

Last week closed with two builders, one storm, and the doing of the Father's will as the verb that separated the wise house from the wrecked one. Poieo. The doing was the diagnostic. But the Sermon on the Mount never said the wise builder would not slip, and the whole canon that is honest about who we are knows that even a builder who has dug to bedrock will need to come back. Salah, the Hebrew verb the Old Testament uses only of God forgiving (we heard Daniel cry it at the close of his prayer three weeks ago in Daniel 9:19), walks straight to the center of the room this week. Last week's poieo was the verb the storm sorted by. This week's two verbs are the ones that meet the builder on the morning after the storm. Repentance is the human side. Forgiveness is the divine side. The cross is where they meet.

🗺️ Historical & Cultural Context
At the Foot of the Mountain

The covenant was six weeks old. Israel had walked through the Red Sea on a Wednesday, eaten the first manna on a Thursday, watched Mount Sinai catch fire under the voice of God at the end of the same month, and stood at the base of the mountain swearing aloud (Exod 24:7) that everything the LORD had spoken they would do. Then Moses went up the mountain to receive the tablets, and forty days passed, and the people decided he was dead. Aaron, the high priest in waiting, melted their gold earrings, fashioned a calf, built an altar, and called for a festival to the LORD. By the time Moses came down with the two stone tablets in his hands, the camp was in full pagan worship of the very God who had just brought them out. He smashed the tablets at the base of the mountain. The book of Exodus calls what happened at that altar covenant adultery on the wedding night.

What happens in the next three chapters is the most theologically loaded sequence in the entire Old Testament. In Exodus 32 the calf is ground to powder, scattered on water, and made to be drunk. Moses' "blot me out of Your book" intercession is recorded on the page beside it. In Exodus 33 the LORD says He will not go up with this people but will send an angel ahead, and Moses asks the question every betrayer has wanted to ask: show me Your glory. And in Exodus 34 God answers it. New tablets are cut, the man climbs the mountain a second time, and the LORD walks past him in the cleft of the rock saying His own name in seven Hebrew words. The Bible's own working definition of who God is comes from a chapter set inside the worst betrayal Israel ever committed. The rest of the canon will keep quoting it. Psalm 86, Psalm 103, Psalm 145, Joel, Jonah, Nahum, Nehemiah will all reach back to Exodus 34:6-7 as if it were the family motto. The hardest day in Israel's history is the day God told the world His name.

📖 This Week's Memory Text
1 John 1:9
☩ Open in Bible App
If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.

One short sentence, written by a fisherman near the end of his life, to people who had started acting like the message of Jesus didn't need to mention sin anymore. The Greek word behind "confess" is homologeo, and it literally means to say the same thing. Confessing sin means calling it what God calls it, not a softer word, not a fancy word, the same word. And notice the two words John picks for how God responds: faithful and just. Not "kind," not "easygoing." Those are courtroom words. God doesn't forgive us by looking the other way on justice. He forgives us because the bill was already paid somewhere else, at the cross. Confession doesn't earn the forgiveness, it just receives what the cross already bought, and the verse promises God washes us all the way clean.

The six days this week are arranged around that one sentence. Isaiah 61:10 gives us a bride dressed in a robe of righteousness, centuries before there's a wedding to wear it to. Hosea 6 is a prophet calling an unfaithful people home, talking like a doctor instead of a judge ("Come, let us return to the LORD"). Acts 3:18-19 is Peter telling the same crowd that had Jesus killed that turning back brings "times of refreshing from the Lord." Exodus 34:1-10 is God saying His own name out loud in the middle of Israel's worst betrayal. Romans 6:23 puts the paycheck of sin next to the free gift of God in one line. And Matthew 22:1-14 closes the week with a king throwing a wedding feast and one guest who couldn't be bothered to put on the robe the king was handing out at the door.

✝️ The Christ Connection

The two words John picks for God in the Memory Text are the words that only make sense at the cross. Faithful, because God promised long before Sinai that He would not abandon the people He had called. Just, because His holiness could not simply wave the wage of sin away as if it had not been earned. Both words sit inside one sentence about forgiveness, and both words sit inside one cross. By Wednesday this week the LORD will be proclaiming His own name in Exodus 34 to a man kneeling beside a freshly cut tablet, and the words He says ("merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and truth, keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity, transgression, and sin") will be the same words Paul will later use to describe what God did at Calvary. By Thursday the parable Jesus tells will turn on a robe a king has provided at His own expense for a guest who has nothing of his own to wear. That robe is Him. The cross is the place the demand of God's justice was satisfied and the offer of God's mercy was extended, and what the rest of the week will keep saying in different words is that we do not bring our own clothing to that table. He hands it to us at the door.

✅ Personal Application Challenge
Read Exodus 34:6-7 aloud once today, slowly, and underline (in your physical Bible or on a card you carry through the week) the seven words the LORD uses to describe Himself: merciful, gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love, abounding in truth, keeping mercy, forgiving. Then write 1 John 1:9 on the same card and tape it where your eyes will land on it each morning before Sunday. Ask one question every time you read the card: "What part of the week ahead am I quietly hoping God will be a different God than the one who named Himself in Exodus 34?" Carry the card into the week. The next six days are going to test whether we actually believe the name He gave to Moses inside the worst betrayal Israel ever committed, and this verse is the heartbeat underneath the whole conversation.
🙏 Opening Prayer for the Week

Father, we come into this week as Aaron's people, prone to melting our gold into something we can see, prone to deciding by lunchtime on Friday that You are dead because we cannot find You where we last left You. Forgive us. Teach us, this week, to say the same word about our sin that You say about it. Not a softer word. Not a clinical word. The same word. Show us the Cross as the place Your justice and Your mercy met without one of them having to lose. Help us hear, by Wednesday, the seven words You spoke aloud to Moses inside the wreckage of the calf, and let those words become the soil we plant the rest of our year in. By Thursday, when the wedding banquet is set and the door is open, do not let us wander in wearing our own clothes. Hand us the robe Your Son already paid for. And when the week is over, send us back into our streets with a tongue that has learned to say "I have sinned" without flinching and a face that has learned to look up because we know what name You gave to Moses. We need You. In Jesus' name, Amen.

💬 Opening Question for the Week
"Where in your life this year have you quietly traded the messy work of repentance for a tidier substitute? (Self-criticism that never names the actual sin out loud. A vague spiritual heaviness that never lands on a specific act. A long apology to a person that never quite admits what the apology is for. A New Year's resolution-style turn that depends on you, not on Him.) What would it look like this week to let the Memory Text's if clause become the actual shape of how you talk to God on Wednesday morning?"
