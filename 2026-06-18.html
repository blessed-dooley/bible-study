Bring Them Back

Growing in a Relationship with God Lesson 12, Share Him Part 6 of 7
June 18, 2026
✦ ☩ ✦
📌 TL;DR

At a crowded watering hole where three flocks have mixed together, one shepherd whistles, and only his own sheep lift their heads. That is the picture God chooses in Zechariah for how He brings wanderers home. He whistles, and they know the call. Today we watch the whistle work on one man: Jesus prays for Peter before the fall, keeps loving him through it, and cooks him breakfast on the other side. We have all been the wanderer. Now we get to be part of the welcome. God is still whistling for His scattered ones, and your consistent love is part of the call.

🔁 Connection to Yesterday

Yesterday we stood at a grave beside the deportation road and heard God answer a weeping mother. His insides churn for His wayward boy, He hears regret muttered in far countries, and He signs the promise twice: they will come back from the enemy's land. But the promise raises today's question, the practical one we live with in the meantime. What do we do while we wait? How do we treat the wanderer across the table at Thanksgiving, the one whose choices still frustrate us, the one who knows all our arguments by heart? Today God shows us His method for bringing them back, and then hands us our part in it. It turns out we were all brought back the same way.

🗺️ Historical & Cultural Context
The Shepherd Who Whistles

At a watering hole in the Judean hills, two or three flocks would crowd together at midday until nobody could tell whose sheep were whose. The shepherds never worried. When it was time to move, each one gave his own call, a piped note, a particular whistle, a cry the flock had heard since lambing season, and out of the woolly chaos his own sheep would separate themselves and follow him. Travelers in the Middle East described the scene for centuries with the same astonishment. Nobody sorts the sheep. The call sorts the sheep.

Zechariah prophesied around 520 BC to a Jerusalem that felt like an un-sorted flock. A remnant had returned from Babylon two decades earlier, but the city was half-built, the temple was a construction site, and most of God's people were still scattered across the empire from Egypt to Assyria, settled into foreign neighborhoods, raising children who had never seen Judah. Into that discouragement Zechariah 10 speaks a promise built on the watering-hole scene every hearer knew: "I will whistle for them and gather them, for I have redeemed them." Not round them up. Not drag them home. Whistle, the way a shepherd calls sheep that know him, and they will come from the far countries the way they once followed him out of the noon-day crowd. God's method for bringing back the scattered has never been force. It is a call they recognize, from a Shepherd they remember.

📖 Into the Word

Be honest about your own story for a moment. There was a season when your heart wandered or cooled, a valley when you were lukewarm for longer than you would like to admit. What brought you back? Hold your answer next to today's anchor, Zechariah 10, where God promises to strengthen, save, restore, and gather His scattered people "because I have compassion on them," as though He had never turned them away. Then watch that whole chapter happen to one fisherman, and hear what it asks of us.

Luke 22:31-32
☩ Open in Bible App
31Simon, Simon, Satan has asked to sift all of you like wheat. 32But I have prayed for you, Simon, that your faith will not fail. And when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers.”

Hours before Peter's worst night, Jesus already knows everything: the courtyard, the servant girl, the rooster, all three denials. The Greek word for "sift," siniazo, pictures grain thrown violently against a screen until everything comes apart, and that is exactly what is coming for Peter's confidence. But look at what Jesus does with His foreknowledge. He does not build a fence around Peter, and He does not write him off. He prays for him, in advance, by name. And then He says "when you have turned back," not "if." Jesus speaks about the wanderer's return as settled fact while the wandering is still in the future. That is how heaven talks about your loved one. Our prayers for them are not wishful thinking thrown at the ceiling. They are joining a prayer Jesus started first.

John 21:15-17
☩ Open in Bible App
15When they had finished eating, Jesus asked Simon Peter, “Simon son of John, do you love Me more than these?” “Yes, Lord,” he answered, “You know I love You.” Jesus replied, “Feed My lambs.” 16Jesus asked a second time, “Simon son of John, do you love Me?” “Yes, Lord,” he answered, “You know I love You.” Jesus told him, “Shepherd My sheep.” 17Jesus asked a third time, “Simon son of John, do you love Me?” Peter was deeply hurt that Jesus had asked him a third time, “Do you love Me?” “Lord, You know all things,” he replied. “You know I love You.” Jesus said to him, “Feed My sheep.

Watch how Jesus treats a wanderer who has just come back. No lecture. No probation period. No "we need to talk about what happened." He cooks breakfast on a charcoal fire, the same kind of fire Peter denied Him beside, and asks one question three times, once for each denial, not to rub it in but to let Peter overwrite each failure with love spoken out loud. Then, astonishingly, He puts the flock in the failure's hands. Feed My lambs. Shepherd My sheep. Jesus restores Peter not just to forgiveness but to usefulness, exactly as Luke 22:32 promised: when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers. If someone you love comes back from the far country, this is the template. Breakfast, not interrogation. A future, not a file of their past.

Ephesians 3:17-19
☩ Open in Bible App
17so that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith. Then you, being rooted and grounded in love, 18will have power, together with all the saints, to comprehend the length and width and height and depth 19of the love of Christ, and to know this love that surpasses knowledge, that you may be filled with all the fullness of God.

Here is where our part becomes possible. Paul prays that believers would be rooted in a love so big it takes four dimensions to measure and still "surpasses knowledge," and that we would be filled with God's own fullness. That filling is what the wanderer in your life actually needs to see, because the frustration, sadness, and judgment we naturally feel toward them will leak out unless something else is filling us first. This is why living from the overflow of daily time with your Savior is not optional in this work. People who have rejected every argument can still be undone by a peace that passes understanding and a love that will not let go. The most persuasive case for the gospel has never been a debate point. It is a life so warmed through by His love that people want to stand near it.

🎯 Street Level
The Name Only Home Uses

You are two thousand miles from where you grew up, reading labels in a grocery store aisle, when a voice somewhere behind you says the name. Not your legal name. The other one. "Junebug?" Your body turns before your brain has voted, because maybe four people alive use that name, and every one of them sat at the same kitchen table you did. A stranger could read your full name off a clipboard in that store and you would feel nothing. The legal name is information. The home name is recognition. Nobody ever taught you to turn around when you hear it. The turning was installed years ago, over suppers and bedtimes and long car rides, by people who loved you before you could spell.

That is how God gathers wanderers. Zechariah says He whistles, the way a shepherd calls sheep that learned his sound as lambs, and when heaven went after Peter, the message on resurrection morning said "tell the disciples and Peter." His name, added on purpose. Junebug, across a far country. Our part in someone's return is not winning the argument, because nobody was ever argued into turning around. Our part is staying part of the sound of home. The no-agenda text, the warmth that does not keep score, the welcome that cooks breakfast instead of opening a file, all of it keeps the home name alive in their ears. The Shepherd is already whistling. Keep their name warm until they turn.

🌍 Today's World, Ancient Truth
The Job That Requires a Past

There is a job in American health care with a strange requirement on the application. You must have fallen. The certified peer support specialist is a person in recovery from mental illness or addiction who is trained, credentialed, and hired to walk beside people still in the middle of it, and the lived experience of collapse and comeback is not a liability on the resume. It is the qualification. SAMHSA, the federal behavioral health agency, defines peer support workers as people who have walked the road of recovery themselves and now help others entering it, and it credits them with something the credentialed system could not supply on its own: through shared understanding, respect, and mutual empowerment, peers help people stay engaged in the recovery process and make relapse less likely. Something happens when the person reaching for you has personally been where you are. The system did not just forgive their past. It put their past to work.

Jesus wrote that job description on a beach two thousand years ago. "When you have turned back, strengthen your brothers" was spoken to Peter before the man had even fallen, and after the resurrection Jesus handed the flock to the disciple with the most public failure on his record. Not forgiveness with a desk in the corner. Feed My lambs. The church's first great shepherd was a certified peer, a man whose denial became the very thing that let him strengthen the shaky. That is the standard for how we welcome wanderers home: a restored person is not a liability to be managed but a witness in training. The comeback is not finished until the brought-back one is trusted with sheep.

✝️ The Christ Connection

The whistling Shepherd of Zechariah 10 has a face. Jesus said His sheep "hear his voice... and the sheep follow him because they know his voice" (John 10:3-4), and then He went out and proved how far the Shepherd will walk to gather one stray. He prayed for Peter before the fall. He turned and looked at him with love in the courtyard at the worst moment of the denial. He sent the women on resurrection morning with a message addressed to "the disciples and Peter," two words that must have hit that broken man like sunrise. And He built a fire on a beach and gave His wanderer back his calling. Every move in that sequence is the gospel in miniature, because every one of us was the sheep He came back for. When we keep loving our wanderers with that kind of patient, unforced, future-giving love, we are not just hoping they return. We are making the Shepherd's whistle audible in a voice they already trust.

✅ Personal Application Challenge
Take five minutes alone today and name out loud the feelings you actually carry toward your wanderer: the sadness, the judgment, the frustration, the helplessness. Hand each one to God specifically ("Lord, take the resentment about Christmas"). Then pray Jesus' own prayer over them by name: "Father, let their faith not fail, and when they turn back, use them to strengthen others." Finish by asking God to fill you so full of His love today that what leaks out of you this week looks like breakfast on the beach, not a court date.
🙏 Closing Prayer

Father, thank You that every one of us praying this prayer is a brought-back wanderer. Somewhere in our story You whistled, and something in us knew the call. Now we pray for the ones still in the far country. Keep praying for them, Lord Jesus, the way You prayed for Peter, and let their faith not fail underneath everything that is sifting them. Empty our hands of the judgment and the frustration we keep picking back up. Root us so deep in Your love, the love too wide and long and high and deep to measure, that what spills out of us is peace they cannot explain and warmth they cannot argue with. Make our lives part of Your whistle. And when they turn toward home, make us the first ones running with breakfast. In Jesus' name, Amen.
