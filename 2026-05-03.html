Faithful Daniel

Growing in a Relationship with God Lesson 6, Prayer Warriors Part 2 of 7
May 3, 2026
✦ ☩ ✦
📌 TL;DR

Daniel kneels at his open window in Babylon three times a day because his prayer life was already an ancient habit by the time the lions appeared on the horizon.

  • ☩ Daniel's most famous prayer is not the lion's den prayer. It is the song he sang the night God revealed Nebuchadnezzar's dream.
  • ☩ The same teenager who refused the king's wine grew into the elder statesman who refused the king's edict; the practice never changed, only the danger did.
  • ☩ Three times a day, knees on the floor, windows cracked toward Jerusalem, while every other senior official in the kingdom watched and waited for him to slip.
  • ☩ The character was forged before it was tested; the window did not become brave on the day of the edict.
🔁 Connection to Yesterday

Yesterday named three teachers and promised a week of leaning on the inclined ear. Today the first teacher steps forward, and he is older than we expected. Daniel was a teenage exile when his story began. By the time the famous prayer at the window happens, he has been praying through three Babylonian kings and the rise of the Medo-Persian empire. The lion's den is not the climax of a sudden burst of courage; it is the natural ripple of a man whose prayer life has been on autopilot for fifty years. The Memory Text said, "I will call upon Him as long as I live." Daniel is what that sentence looks like over five decades of evidence.

🗺️ Historical & Cultural Context
The Statesman Who Outlasted the Empires

Picture the floorplan of Daniel's room in Babylon. It is the second story of a residence prominent enough that the windows can be seen from the street. The window facing southwest, toward the rubble of Solomon's temple seven hundred miles away, is the one that mattered. He opens it three times a day even after the king signs an edict that turns the open window into a death sentence. The watchers below have been waiting for years to find one fault in him. They will not find a moral failure, so they go after his religion.

Daniel was deported to Babylon around 605 BC, somewhere between fifteen and eighteen years old. By the time we meet him in chapter six, he has watched four kings (Nebuchadnezzar, Belshazzar, Darius, and Cyrus on the way) and lived through the takeover that ended the Babylonian empire entirely. The cities he has seen burn, the gods he has watched topple, the courts he has navigated, none of it scrambled the schedule. The Bible's repeated descriptors of him read less like a résumé and more like the scent rising off a lifetime of hours at the open window: a teenager who refused to defile himself with the king's diet in Daniel 1:8 and was given understanding in dreams in Daniel 1:17; an interpreter in whom the Spirit of the holy God was visible to four different kings in Daniel 4:9, Daniel 4:18, and Daniel 5:14; an old man called "greatly beloved" by Heaven itself in Daniel 9:23 and Daniel 10:11. The character was forged before it was tested.

📖 Into the Word

By the time the king signs the death decree in chapter six, Daniel has been praying with the window open for somewhere north of fifty years. The schedule is older than the threat. Two prayers in particular sit at opposite ends of his life and the same testimony runs through both: a man with a quiet, abiding connection with God learns to talk to Him about everything. The thanksgiving in chapter two and the kneeling in chapter six are not different practices. They are the same conversation in two different rooms.

Daniel 2:20-23
☩ Open in Bible App
20and declared: “Blessed be the name of God forever and ever, for wisdom and power belong to Him. 21He changes the times and seasons; He removes kings and establishes them. He gives wisdom to the wise and knowledge to the discerning. 22He reveals the deep and hidden things; He knows what lies in darkness, and light dwells with Him. 23To You, O God of my fathers, I give thanks and praise, because You have given me wisdom and power. And now You have made known to me what we have requested, for You have made known to us the dream of the king.”

This is what Daniel sounds like before the danger has fully passed. King Nebuchadnezzar has decreed that every wise man in Babylon will be executed unless someone can not only interpret his dream but tell him what the dream was. Daniel has gathered his three friends and asked them to plead for mercy with him in Daniel 2:18. God has answered by giving him the dream and its meaning in a night vision. The natural human move would be to run to the king with the answer before someone changed his mind. Daniel runs in the other direction. He stops and sings.

The Aramaic word behind "blessed" (*berakh*) means literally to bend the knee. Daniel is bowing in his thanksgiving even before he stands to speak in court. He thanks God for two things first, and the order is telling. He blesses God for who He is (wisdom and might belong to Him; He changes times and seasons; He removes kings and raises up kings) before he thanks God for what He just received (the dream's meaning). The personal favor is third in the list, which is exactly the right place for it. He is thanking the God who outranks Nebuchadnezzar in front of an audience that knows only Nebuchadnezzar.

Notice what "wise men" meant in this culture. Daniel had been one of the official wise men of Babylon since the king's selection program described in Daniel 1:20, where he and his three friends emerged ten times wiser than the rest. He had survived Arioch's sword in Daniel 2:14 only because he asked for the time the king refused to give. By Daniel 2:21 and Daniel 2:23, Daniel is calling that wisdom by its right name, a gift that belongs to God. By Daniel 2:48, he is the second-most powerful man in the empire and still has the same posture: knees bent inside the title. The position never changed.

Daniel 6:10-11
☩ Open in Bible App
10Now when Daniel learned that the document had been signed, he went into his house, where the windows of his upper room opened toward Jerusalem, and three times a day he got down on his knees, prayed, and gave thanks to his God, just as he had done before. 11Then these men went as a group and found Daniel petitioning and imploring his God.

Sixty years separate this prayer from the one in chapter two. Daniel is no longer a teenager in a wisdom academy. He is a senior official under three different empires, and the latest king has signed an edict that bans private prayer to anyone except the king himself for thirty days. Daniel learns that the document has been signed. The next sentence does not say he debated. It says he went home.

What Daniel does next is the practice of fifty years before it is a defiance. He climbs the stairs to the upper room. He opens the windows, which are already cracked. He kneels, which is the position he prays in three times a day every day, and has been since he was a young man. The line that splits the universe is the small phrase, "just as he had done before." Daniel is not breaking out a special prayer for a special threat. He is doing what he was already doing. His character has been described in Daniel 6:3 as having an "excellent spirit" the king was about to promote, and in Daniel 6:4 as having no error or fault that anyone could find. Character so settled that political enemies trying to take him down had to invent a religious crime to catch him in Daniel 6:5-9. The window did not become brave on the day of the edict. The window had been brave for fifty years. Anyone who is praying because today is hard is praying late. Daniel is praying because today is Tuesday.

🎯 Street Level
The Pre-Flight Walkaround

Most travelers never notice that the pilot has been walking around the plane for the last ten minutes. Out on the tarmac in any weather, somewhere between the cup of coffee and boarding, the captain runs a hand along the leading edge of a wing. He pulls a pin from a pitot tube. He looks at the tires. He kicks one. He signs a piece of paper. Ninety-nine times out of a hundred the walkaround turns up nothing. The plane was already fine. The walkaround is, by any reasonable definition, a waste of ten minutes.

Until the day it isn't. Until the morning he notices a hairline crack in a panel the night crew somehow missed, or a thin glaze of ice on a pitot tube the de-icing truck did not quite catch. On those mornings the entire flight, every passenger and every soul on the ground beneath the takeoff path, owes their afternoon to a habit that had been boring for fifteen years.

That walkaround is Daniel's window. The kneeling he did the morning the edict was signed was not invented in that moment. It was the practice that had been turning up nothing for fifty years. The schedule did not become brave on the day of the decree. It had been kneeling on every uneventful Tuesday before it. The captain who climbs the stairs on the day a real problem is on board is not braver than he was yesterday. He is just still doing what he has been doing.

🌍 Today's World, Ancient Truth
The Routine That Was Already Running

Watch a closer in the bottom of the ninth take the mound for the seventy-second time that season. Before the first pitch he taps the rosin bag twice, scrapes a line in the dirt with his cleat, exhales once through the nose. The crowd is on its feet. The runner on second is the tying run. He throws strike one, painted on the outside corner. None of the cameras are looking at the part that mattered. The tap, the scrape, the exhale were all rehearsed in spring training in February when nothing was on the line. The body learned the sequence then. The crowd is being shown the result tonight.

A 2021 meta-analysis in International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology pooled the studies on what sports scientists call "pre-performance routines" and found a moderate-to-large effect size on performance specifically under pressurised conditions. A 2025 qualitative study in the Journal of Applied Sport Psychology interviewed seventeen athletes shortly after they delivered a clutch moment in real competition, and the pattern across every interview was the same: the clutch moment was not improvised. It was the routine that had been turning up nothing for months, running on automatic when the threat finally arrived. Researchers call the mechanism automaticity, the freeing of conscious attention because the body has already memorized the sequence. Athletes call it not having to think. The vocabulary differs. The point is identical.

Daniel's window was a pre-performance routine for a moment he could not have known was coming. By the time the edict was signed, the kneeling, the directional facing, the morning-noon-and-evening rhythm had been automatic for half a century. He did not have to summon courage in chapter six. He had to keep doing what his knees already knew. Anyone hoping to be brave under pressure tomorrow without having practiced today is hoping for what the literature does not record. The schedule is the only training program for the threat. Daniel had been running it since he was fifteen.

✝️ The Christ Connection

Sixty years of kneeling at the same window is one of the dimmest reflections of the kneeling that defined Jesus' life. The Gospels record His pre-dawn departures, His evenings on a mountain, His all-night vigils before the calling of the twelve, His three returns to the same olive grove on the night before the cross. He was the Man whose prayer life had no edges. The Man Daniel pointed toward did what Daniel did every day, only without an empty seat at the head of the conversation. The Father had always been there, and the Son had always been speaking. When Hebrews calls Jesus a High Priest who "always lives to make intercession," it means that the kneeling has never stopped. There is, right now, someone praying for you whose practice did not begin when the trouble started. He has been praying for you since before the lions ever knew you existed.

✅ Personal Application Challenge
Pick a window. Today, find the window in your house, your car, or your office where you can see daylight, and stand in front of it for one minute before noon. Use it for one sentence to God: thanksgiving for one specific thing He has done in the last week, and one specific request for someone who needs Him this week. Daniel had three windows a day. You only need one to begin.
🙏 Closing Prayer

Father, we want a prayer life like Daniel's, and we know we cannot order one on demand. We confess that our prayers have too often been emergency calls instead of daily conversation. We have prayed when the lions were already in the room and called it faith. Forgive us. Teach us to begin praying when nothing is on fire. Teach us to thank You before we even understand what You have given us. Teach us to come to the same window day after day, until our knees know the floor and the floor knows our knees. And when the edicts come, as they will, let us already be in the upper room with the windows open. We are not asking for new courage. We are asking for the slow kind, the kind that grows in soil watered for fifty years. In Jesus' name, Amen.
