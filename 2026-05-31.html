The Rush of Life

Growing in a Relationship with God Lesson 10, Repentance and Forgiveness Part 2 of 7
May 31, 2026
✦ ☩ ✦
📌 TL;DR

A woman wakes early on a Sabbath she loves more than any other day, and by ten in the morning the laundry is sorted, the banana bread is in the oven, the dress shirt is ironed, and she has not yet sat down.

  • ☩Repentance does not start with a list of sins on a card. It starts with a moment of vision, the Spirit putting one foot through the kitchen floor and showing us where we have been walking.
  • ☩Martha is on her feet in Bethany, scandalized that her sister is doing nothing. Jesus calls Mary's nothing the one thing necessary, and the word He uses for it is the word for the best portion at the table.
  • ☩Isaiah uses the strongest possible Hebrew word for what our best efforts look like on the divine scale. Zechariah watches the angel hand a clean robe to a man who came to court in stained clothes.
  • ☩We do not sew our own wedding garment. The Bride in Isaiah 61 has been clothed; she did not choose the cloth, did not stitch the hem, did not arrange the fit. The robe was put on her.
🔁 Connection to Yesterday

Yesterday closed with the LORD walking past Moses in the cleft of the rock and saying His own name aloud as "merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and truth." The whole rest of the Old Testament will keep quoting that sentence. But the seven words God spoke on that mountain do not become a key in our hand unless we first realize the door we are standing outside of. Today is the first turn of the lock. The Memory Text's if clause ("if we confess our sins") cannot be honest until we have seen the sin clearly enough to say a word about it. Repentance has a beginning, and the beginning is sight. A woman on a Sabbath morning, an iron in one hand, is going to see for one second what her week has been doing to her, and that one second is the seed of everything the rest of the week will harvest.

🗺️ Historical & Cultural Context
The Disciple's Posture That Was Not Hers To Take

Bethany sits about two miles east of Jerusalem on the back slope of the Mount of Olives, the last village a traveler from the Jordan Valley passes before climbing the ridge into the holy city. It is so close to Jerusalem that Pilgrim crowds at Passover often slept there because the city itself was full. Bethany is where Jesus stayed when He came to Jerusalem for the festivals, in the house of Lazarus and his two sisters, the household John's gospel will later record as "the one Jesus loved." Hosting Jesus was not a small thing. When the rabbi arrived He almost always arrived with the twelve, and likely more, and the female head of the household was responsible for everything between the door and the table. Martha is doing in Luke 10 what every woman in first-century Judea was raised to do. The expectations were not Martha's invention; they were the air she breathed.

What Mary is doing is the cultural scandal. Sitting at a rabbi's feet is the disciple's posture. It is what Paul says he did "at the feet of Gamaliel" in Acts 22:3, the formal academic phrase for studying for the rabbinate. First-century rabbinic culture did not seat women in that posture. The training was male, the seminary was male, the floor was male. Mary takes the floor anyway. Martha's complaint is not just about dishes; it is about a sister who has crossed a social line. Jesus answers her in the gentlest way, but He does not move Mary off the floor. The good part He commends is the disciple's part, and He has just authorized a woman to take it.

📖 Into the Word

The morning the woman in our opening scene has been having is the morning Martha had two thousand years ago. Today walks through the kitchen with her and watches the moment her hand stops moving. Then it walks her, and us, through the verses that name what just happened and offer the only trade that can answer it. These verses set the Martha story next to Isaiah's image of our best efforts on the divine scale and Isaiah's image of the robe God Himself provides. The order is not accidental. Vision first. Then the exchange.

Luke 10:41-42
☩ Open in Bible App
41“Martha, Martha,” the Lord replied, “you are worried and upset about many things. 42But only one thing is necessary. Mary has chosen the good portion, and it will not be taken away from her.”

Jesus says her name twice, the way you'd repeat someone's name softly right before you tell them something hard but kind. He's not mad at Martha. Her kitchen was full of love. But He sees what she can't. The Greek word Luke uses for what Mary chose is meris, and it means the best portion of food, the plate you set in front of the guest of honor. Mary picked the best thing on the table, and it was just being close to Him. Martha gets called "distracted" in Luke 10:40, a word that means she's being pulled in circles, yanked every direction while the one thing that actually mattered was sitting in the next room. That's where repentance starts. Not with some sin Martha was committing, because she wasn't, but with the gap she couldn't see until Jesus pointed right at it. You put the dishes down, look at your own week, and you see it too.

Isaiah 64:6
☩ Open in Bible App
Each of us has become like something unclean, and all our righteous acts are like filthy rags; we all wither like a leaf, and our iniquities carry us away like the wind.

When the prophet says even our best behavior looks like "filthy rags," he's not talking about the sins we already feel bad about. He means our good stuff, the things we're proud of. The clean house. The ironed shirt. The banana bread baking for the people coming over. He grabs the strongest word Hebrew has for something filthy, and his point isn't that doing good is bad. It's that trying to use our good works to earn our way to God just doesn't work. He says we "dry up like a leaf," brittle and ready to blow away in the next gust of wind. Sunday morning is the morning we finally admit he's right, because the very next verse is about to show us what God has been holding onto the whole time.

Isaiah 61:10
☩ Open in Bible App
I will rejoice greatly in the LORD, my soul will exult in my God; for He has clothed me with garments of salvation and wrapped me in a robe of righteousness, as a bridegroom wears a priestly headdress, as a bride adorns herself with her jewels.

Look at who's doing the work in this verse. God clothes her, God wraps her. The bride doesn't pick the fabric, sew the seams, or get it fitted. She just finds herself already dressed, in a robe that back then meant royalty and priesthood. It was a sign of rank, and it was simply handed to her. Zechariah watched this same thing happen to a real man. The high priest Joshua is standing in court in filthy clothes, and the angel says, "Take off his filthy clothes," then tells him, "I'm going to dress you in fine clothes" (Zechariah 3:4). John saw the final version of this up in heaven, a huge crowd in white robes that had been "washed in the blood of the Lamb" (Revelation 7:14). For a thousand years the Bible keeps showing the same picture. We don't make the robe. We're given it. By Thursday it shows up at a wedding, where the King Himself hands out the garment at the door.

🎯 Street Level
The Recital Footage

Most parents have lived something close to this. The kindergarten or first-grade recital was a month ago. You were there, third row, phone held up the whole time so you could send the video to your mom. You filmed your daughter sing her one solo line. You filmed the small bow. You walked out feeling like a present parent.

Then this morning, for the first time since you sent it, you go back to the video. You watch it on a bigger screen. There is your daughter, in her little costume, hitting her line beautifully. But in the lower corner of the frame, on the lap of the woman in the row in front of you, you can see the reflection in her phone screen of your own face. You are not looking at the stage. You are reading email on your other phone. You filmed yourself missing it.

That is what the Spirit does on a Sunday morning. He does not invent a sin you did not commit. He holds up the footage of the week you actually had. The iron goes down because the picture is not the one you have been carrying in your head. Repentance does not begin with effort. It begins with the moment you finally see what the camera was recording the whole time. Mary in Bethany was sitting inside the footage that mattered. Martha was on her feet, doing everything right, in the same frame, missing it. And the One holding the footage already loves both of them.

🌍 Today's World, Ancient Truth
The Half-Week You Missed

In 2010 the journal Science published a paper by Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert at Harvard that used a then-novel iPhone web app to sample people's lives in real time. Twenty-two hundred and fifty adults across eighty-three countries were pinged at random points during the day and asked, "What are you doing right now? Are you thinking about that, or something else?" The quarter-million resulting data points returned one of the more arresting findings in modern psychology. People spent forty-six and nine-tenths percent of their waking hours mentally somewhere other than the present moment. Half the week was missed by the person actually living it.

A 2024 conceptual replication and extension led by Madeleine Gross and colleagues revisited the question with a younger post-pandemic sample and a sharper instrument, and the headline shifted in two honest ways. The wandering rate they measured was lower than the original 47 percent (closer to a quarter of prompts, which the authors attributed in part to the demographics of the sample), and the mood penalty for wandering, while real and in the same direction, turned out to be considerably smaller than the original paper had suggested. The variable that did most of the work was not whether attention was present or wandering, but the emotional valence of the thought itself. Negative content made the mood worse whether the person was daydreaming about it or rehearsing it in the room. The paper's own conclusion was that the interventions worth pursuing are the ones that change the content of the thought, or anchor it to a chosen object, not just the on-off switch between present and absent.

That is the gap Jesus put a name to in Bethany. Martha was in the kitchen and ten other rooms at the same time. Mary was on the floor with no other room playing in her head. The smartphone-buzzed twenty-first century did not invent the half-attendant life; it just gave the science department a way to measure it. Forty-seven percent is a Martha number. The good part Mary chose was the one thing Martha was not free to take, because the room she was in was not the room her head was in.

✝️ The Christ Connection

The robe in Isaiah 61:10 is not a metaphor for a doctrine. It is a Person. The Bride is clothed because the Bridegroom is willing to be stripped. The robe Joshua the high priest receives in Zechariah 3 is provided because somewhere outside the courtroom a Lamb is being prepared. The white robes in Revelation 7 have been "washed in the blood of the Lamb," and there is only one Lamb whose blood does what that sentence says. Mary's "good part" at His feet was good because the One she was listening to was on His way to the cross, and the cross is the place the wedding garment was made and paid for. Sunday morning a woman in a kitchen sets the iron down because she sees the gap, and the One who shows her the gap is the same One holding the robe. He does not show us the wound He has not already arranged to heal.

✅ Personal Application Challenge
Pick one ten-minute window today, before the sun goes down, and sit. Phone in another room. Bible on your lap or in your hands. Read Isaiah 61:10 aloud, then Isaiah 64:6 aloud, in that order. Do not fix anything in your day during the window. Do not plan tomorrow. Sit at His feet like Mary did, and let the two verses do their slow trade in your chest. If tears come, do not interrupt them. Psalm 53:2 says God looks down to see who has any understanding, who is seeking Him. Today let Him find you sitting still.
🙏 Closing Prayer

Father, we are Martha most weeks of the year, dragged in circles by the urgent things that have crowded out the necessary One. Forgive us. We confess that we have measured our days by what got crossed off, and we have walked past Your feet on the way to the kitchen more mornings than we can count. Show us, this week, that the good part is not a reward for getting our list done. It is the chair we are supposed to be in while the list is still on the counter. Take from us the filthy garment of our self-arranged righteousness. The leaf-thin coat we keep pretending will be warm enough. Wrap us in the robe Your Son already finished for us, the one He bought at His own expense and hands us at the door of this week. Help us, today, to set the iron down for ten minutes and let Him be Jesus in the room. We need You. In Jesus' name, Amen.

💬 Discussion Question
"Where in your week right now are you doing the Martha thing inside a household that genuinely loves Jesus? (A volunteer slot you can't drop. A serving role at church that has started running you. A ministry to your own family that has begun to crowd out the time at His feet that produced the love for them in the first place.) What is the one ten-minute trade you could make this week to sit where Mary sat without quitting where you serve?"
