Real Repentance

Growing in a Relationship with God Lesson 10, Repentance and Forgiveness Part 4 of 7
June 2, 2026
✦ ☩ ✦
📌 TL;DR

A man in camel hair stands knee-deep in the Jordan River shouting one Greek verb at temple-going Jews, and four hundred years of prophetic silence breaks on the same word the rabbi from Nazareth will reach for on the morning He starts His own ministry.

  • ☩The first sermon recorded from John the Baptist and the first sermon recorded from Jesus open with the same verb. Change your mind, turn around. The kingdom is closer than you thought.
  • ☩Biblical repentance has two steps in one motion. Honest sorrow that names the sin out loud, and the decision to walk a different direction from the place we are standing.
  • ☩The engine of the whole thing is not our willpower but God's kindness. Paul tells the Romans that it is the kind treatment of God, not the harsh one, that draws us back.
  • ☩Refreshing. Peter's word for what arrives after the turn is anapsyxis, the cool breath of relief after a long climb. Repentance is not a punishment we serve; it is the door we open to that breath.
🔁 Connection to Yesterday

Yesterday named the Spirit's exposing work, the Parakletos who shows us what we have not yet been willing to see. Today asks what we do when the seeing lands. The answer the Bible gives is a single Greek verb with a two-step shape. Metanoeo. The compound is meta- (after, with) and noeo (mind, perception); literally "to think differently after." But the Hebrew sitting under it is older and more physical: shuv, to turn the body around. The Spirit produces the change of mind. The change of mind walks the feet to a different door. Yesterday was the seeing. Today is the turning.

🗺️ Historical & Cultural Context
The First Word in the Public Story of Jesus

Israel had not heard a prophet for somewhere around four centuries when a man in camel hair walked out of the Judean wilderness, set up next to the Jordan River about fifteen miles east of Jerusalem, and began doing two things first-century Jews did not expect to see together. He preached. And he baptized. The preaching word was old. The baptizing word was the problem. Ritual immersion in a mikveh existed all over Jewish life (every observant Jew immersed before festival days, after childbirth, after contact with the dead), and Gentile converts were required to immerse when they entered the covenant. What John was doing was different. He was telling Jewish people, fully inside the covenant, that their religion was not enough on its own. They needed to come into the river the same way a Gentile would, as if they were starting their relationship with God from the beginning.

That was scandalous, and the temple knew it. The Pharisees came down to ask him what he thought he was doing. John's answer, recorded in Matthew 3:1-2, was one imperative verb: metanoeite. Then Jesus walked down to the same river, asked to be baptized, and a few weeks later began His own ministry in Galilee with the same word. Mark 1:14-15 records it: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand. Repent and believe in the gospel." The first public sentence of the gospel from both directions was the same imperative. Repentance is not a footnote tucked at the back of the Christian message. It is the doorway. By the time Peter is preaching in Solomon's portico after the resurrection (Luke 24:46-47 says Jesus Himself laid down the pattern in the upper room: that "repentance for the forgiveness of sins should be preached in His name to all nations"), the inaugural word has become the worldwide one.

📖 Into the Word

Peter is preaching the second sermon of the church on Solomon's portico after the lame man at the Beautiful Gate has been healed in the name of Jesus. The crowd is large; the priests are coming. The anchor today is the two sentences Peter says about repentance in front of that crowd, the kindness clause Paul writes to the Romans about what powers it, and the warning Jesus pairs with the gospel of John's vine. Three angles on one Greek verb. By the close of the section we will see why both John in the Jordan and Jesus in Galilee opened with the same word.

Acts 3:18-19
☩ Open in Bible App
18But in this way God has fulfilled what He foretold through all the prophets, saying that His Christ would suffer. 19Repent, then, and turn back, so that your sins may be wiped away,

Peter uses two action words back to back. First, change your mind. Then, turn around, the word a ship's pilot uses to swing the boat onto a new heading. That's the two-part shape of repentance: honestly admitting what's wrong, then actually walking a different direction. And Peter knew the route, because this is the same man who had denied Jesus three times and still found his way back. Look at what's waiting on the other side of the turn. Your sins get "wiped out," the way you erase ink off a page or stamp a debt paid in full. And then come "times of refreshing," a Greek word, anapsyxis, that shows up only once in the whole New Testament and means the cool breath you finally take at the top of a long hill. Notice the order, too. Christ suffered, therefore repent. The repentance doesn't pay for your sin, because the cross already did that. Repentance is just the open hand reaching out for what's already been bought.

Romans 2:4
☩ Open in Bible App
Or do you disregard the riches of His kindness, tolerance, and patience, not realizing that God’s kindness leads you to repentance?

The word Paul uses for God's "kindness" here isn't mild niceness. It's kindness that costs the giver something, kindness with skin in the game. Paul stacks two more words next to it: God holding back a hand that has every right to come down, and God being "long-tempered," the exact opposite of a short fuse. Then the sentence does something beautiful. That kindness "leads" us to repentance, and the word for leads is the one a shepherd uses to gently guide the flock, not the prod a driver jabs them with. So the thing that actually turns us around isn't fear or shame. It's getting to experience how kind God is. Revelation 3:19 says Jesus corrects the people He loves and tells a lukewarm church to turn back, so the discipline and the love are the same thing. And 2 Peter 3:9 says the Lord isn't slow about His promise, He's patient, "not wanting any to perish." What looks like God being slow is really God holding the door open one more year.

John 15:2
☩ Open in Bible App
He cuts off every branch in Me that bears no fruit, and every branch that does bear fruit, He prunes to make it even more fruitful.

Here Jesus shows a second tool in the Gardener's hand: the pruning knife. Pruning sounds harsh, but it's the careful cut a grower makes on a healthy branch so it will grow even more fruit, and in Greek the word for pruning is a close cousin of the word for "clean." Pruning is cleansing. Repentance is the moment we let the Gardener cut away whatever is keeping us from becoming what we could be. The fruit He's after is named a few verses later, a life that points other people to the Father (John 15:8). John the Baptist used the same picture, telling people to "bear fruit that proves your repentance" (Matthew 3:8). And Paul boiled the whole thing down to "repentance toward God and faith in our Lord Jesus" (Acts 20:21), two things that are really one motion, like the two chambers of a single heart. The shears are kind, and the cutting brings life, which is exactly how the first church described it: God "granted repentance that leads to life" (Acts 11:18). 1 John 1:9 says it in even fewer words.

🎯 Street Level
The Plow at the Headland

A farmer plowing a field with a moldboard plow runs into a shape problem every farmer in human history has had to solve. The plow only cuts going one direction. It turns the soil over to one side, and when the tractor reaches the end of the row, there is a strip of unplowed ground at the edge of the field called the headland. The farmer cannot just keep going. He cannot just feel bad about how the row turned out. He has to stop the tractor, lift the plow out of the ground, swing the rig in a wide arc around the headland, drop the plow back in on the new line, and head back across the field in the opposite direction.

The turn is not the failure. The turn is the whole shape of the work. Without it, the field is half-plowed. Without it, every furrow ends at a wall. A farmer who only knows how to drive in a straight line is not a farmer; he is a tractor operator about to drive into the fence. The skill is the headland turn. Watch a farmer's son in his first April: the dad will let him drive the straight rows. The dad does the turn himself until the boy's hands are ready.

Biblical repentance is the headland turn. Sorrow without the turn is a tractor idling at the end of the row, the driver crying about the row he just plowed without lifting the plow or swinging the rig. Direction-change without sorrow is a tractor operator who has never noticed where the row actually ended. The verb the Old Testament gives us, shuv, is the farm verb. Lift, turn, lower, head back. Anapsyxis, the cool breath Peter promised, is what fills your lungs as the rig comes back around and you can see the next row laid out clean ahead of you. The field is not finished. It is still being worked. But the turn has happened, and the next furrow has already started.

🌍 Today's World, Ancient Truth
The Argument That Moved Recidivism

In May 2025 the journal Psychosocial Intervention published a systematic review and meta-analysis by Pinto e Silva, Gouveia, Santirso, Cunha, and Caridade at the University of Minho in Portugal, pooling twenty-two studies on a counseling method called motivational interviewing (MI) and its use with people sentenced for intimate partner violence, violent crime, sexual offenses, drug offenses, and driving offenses. Sample sizes ran from twenty-five to five hundred and twenty-eight across the studies. The treatment was strikingly modest, between one and six conversations with a trained interviewer. The question the meta-analysis asked is the question this Tuesday is asking: when a person needs to stop doing something they have been doing, what actually moves the verb?

The results read like Acts 3:19 in clinical English. MI sessions, the style of counseling that refuses to confront and instead draws out the client's own reasons for change, increased session attendance and reduced dropout compared with the older directive approaches. More importantly, the meta-analysis found a statistically significant reduction in official recidivism for the MI groups. People who had been ordered into the room by a judge did better, often after one to six conversations, when the conversation took the kindness route into the turn instead of the shouting one.

Paul made the same observation in Romans 2:4 with no clinical trials to lean on. The kindness of God leads us to repentance. The 2025 forensic data is a small academic footnote to a sentence God has been speaking out of the Old Testament for three thousand years. Without the harshness, the plow makes its headland turn cleanly and the next furrow opens straight ahead. The voice in the cab is the one that matters.

✝️ The Christ Connection

Peter's "therefore" in Acts 3:19 is the most important word in his sermon. Christ has suffered; therefore repent. The repentance the apostle is calling for is the act of receiving what the suffering already accomplished. The cross is not the consequence the believer pays through enough sorrow to qualify. The cross is the price already paid, and repentance is the open hand the Father has been waiting to fill with what His Son already finished. This is why Paul could call God's chrestotes the engine of the whole thing. The kindness Paul names is the kindness that has skin on it, that wore the crown, that hung between two thieves so a Roman portico crowd one Pentecost morning could be called back. The verb both John and Jesus opened with had the cross built into its grammar from the first time it left their mouths. The Vinedresser of John 15 prunes with shears that have already cut Him. He asks of us nothing He has not already absorbed.

✅ Personal Application Challenge
Take the sentence you wrote on yesterday's piece of paper, the one the Parakletos named, and turn it into the two halves of biblical repentance. Step one, write underneath it the honest sentence: "I am sorry that I [did this specific thing], and I have not been calling it by its real name." Step two, write under that the concrete turn: "Today I will [do this one specific opposite-direction thing] in its place." Then do the second sentence before the day is over. Repentance is not the moment you wrote the words. It is the moment your feet walked the second sentence into a real Tuesday.
🙏 Closing Prayer

Father, the first sermon Your Son ever preached opened on one verb, and we have spent most of our lives running from it. Teach us to hear it the way the Solomon's portico crowd heard it the second time around, with the cross between us and the call. Show us that Your kindness, not Your harshness, is what is leading us toward this turn. We confess that we have offered You cheap sorrow more often than honest one, and tidy apologies more often than the actual change of direction. Forgive us. Make our repentance two-step the way the Bible makes it two-step, sorrow that names the sin and feet that walk a different road. Send us today out of the door of the climb into the cool breath of the refreshing You promised on the other side. We need You. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Or the turn without the sorrow: the behavior change that skips the honest naming of what was actually wrong, the white-knuckle willpower that has never said "I am sorry" out loud.) What would it look like this week to walk both halves of the same step, in the same Tuesday, before the sun goes down?"
