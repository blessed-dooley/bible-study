What Love Does Not Do

The Essence of Christian Life and Witness Lesson 7, A Portrait of Love Part 4 of 7
August 11, 2026
✦ ☩ ✦
📌 TL;DR

Today Paul paints in negative space. Eight things love refuses to do, and every one of them feels good in the moment. The flash of envy at a friend's news. The subtle brag slipped into conversation. The sharp reply that wins the exchange. The tidy mental ledger where every wrong is filed by date. Paul is not listing random vices. Each refusal matches something his own congregation was busy doing. Love is proven less by the impressive things it does and more by the satisfying things it declines.

🔁 Connection to Yesterday

Yesterday you ran one deliberate rep of your hardest verb, patience without the sigh, kindness without the invoice, joy at news that was not yours. Seven verbs showed us what love does with its hands. But Paul knows something about hands. They do damage as naturally as they do good. So today the portrait adds its shadows, eight refusals, the things love will not let itself do no matter how justified they feel. If yesterday was love's job description, today is its code of conduct, and it reads like Paul had been sitting in Corinth's pews taking notes. He had been reading their mail, at least. And ours.

🗺️ Historical & Cultural Context
A Port City Kept Its Books

Stand on the Lechaion Road, Corinth's marble spine running up from the harbor, and listen to the sound of business. A stylus pressing figures into a wax tablet. Grain from Sicily, weighed and entered. A loan to a shipwright, terms and date, entered. Corinth sat on the narrow neck between two seas, and cargo from both directions paid its way across, which made the city one enormous counting house. Fortunes lived and died by the ledgers. A merchant who forgot what he was owed did not stay a merchant, so nothing owed was ever forgotten. Everything was written down, and everything written down was collected.

Paul reaches straight into that world for one of today's refusals. Love, he writes, does not keep a record of wrongs, and the verb he chooses is a bookkeeping term, the word for entering an item into the account so it can be settled later. Every Corinthian knew that motion of the stylus. Paul's claim is that love refuses to run its relationships like a counting house. It keeps no column of injuries, logs no dates beside offenses, carries no receivables file of things to bring up during the next argument. In the best-audited city in Greece, Paul describes a love that deliberately loses track of what it is owed. That was bad accounting. It still is. It is also the only way any relationship survives.

📖 Into the Word

Why would Paul spend half his portrait on negatives? Because the Corinthians could have signed the positive list and gone to lunch. It is the refusals that name names. Read the eight again slowly and notice how precisely they trace this church's file, the envy behind the factions, the puffed-up knowledge, the rudeness of the strong toward the weak, the lawsuits chasing personal rights. Paul re-reads his own list back to a congregation that was living its opposite, and somewhere in the reading, the mirror turns toward us.

1 Corinthians 13:4-7
☩ Open in Bible App
4Love is patient, love is kind. It does not envy, it does not boast, it is not proud. 5It is not rude, it is not self-seeking, it is not easily angered, it keeps no account of wrongs. 6Love takes no pleasure in evil, but rejoices in the truth. 7It bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things.

Count the refusals this time. Love does not envy, does not boast, is not arrogant, is not rude, is not self-seeking, is not irritable, keeps no record of wrongs, and finds no joy when someone else goes wrong. Behind that record-keeping phrase sits the Greek verb logizomai, an accounting word, to enter something into the books. Love refuses the ledger. It also refuses the touchiness that turns every slight into an event, and the smug little lift we feel when a rival stumbles. None of these refusals comes naturally, because each one means giving up something that pays immediately, the win, the credit, the collected debt. That is exactly why they prove love is real. Anyone can perform the positives on a good day. Only love says no to what feels this good.

The first refusal deserves a closer look, because Paul has used its verb positively all through this letter. Desire the greater gifts, he says (1 Corinthians 12:31). Desire spiritual gifts (1 Corinthians 14:1). Desire to prophesy (1 Corinthians 14:39). Same Greek verb, turned toward God's gifts, and it is holy hunger. Turned toward a gifted person, it curdles into the thing that made Joseph's brothers sell him south (Acts 7:9) and the thing splitting Corinth into camps, where envy and strife were walking proof of spiritual infancy (1 Corinthians 3:3). Wanting what God gives is worship. Resenting who God gave it to is war.

1 Corinthians 8:1
☩ Open in Bible App
Now about food sacrificed to idols: We know that we all have knowledge. Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up.

Here is where boasting and arrogance were actually living in Corinth. The educated members knew an idol was nothing, and their knowledge had inflated them until they could no longer see the brother at the next table whose conscience was still tender from a lifetime in the temples (1 Corinthians 8). Paul does not dispute their facts. He disputes their posture, dripping irony about the wise and strong looking down on the fools for Christ (1 Corinthians 4:10). His two verbs tell the whole story. Puffing up inflates one person, a balloon, all air and skin. Building up raises a house other people can live in. Every congregation is doing one or the other, and rudeness towards the weak is simply arrogance with its elbows out.

1 Corinthians 10:24
☩ Open in Bible App
No one should seek his own good, but the good of others.

Love is not self-seeking, and Paul had already given that refusal a rule of its own. Do not chase your own advantage. Chase the other person's. This is the sentence that sounded most insane in Corinth and sounds most insane now, because both cultures run on securing your own. Notice, though, what Paul is actually proposing. Not a world where nobody's needs matter, but a community where everyone's needs are carried by someone else, each member seeking the good of the other until the circle closes and every person in it is looked after. Rights enforced to the wall leave a room full of winners who cannot stand each other. Rights laid down build the one thing Corinth could not buy, a people who actually belong to one another.

🎯 Street Level
You Were Right and You Said Nothing

You told them. Maybe it was the used car with paint too shiny for its mileage, or the plan to fix the roof without calling anyone who fixes roofs, or the guy who was charming the way weather is nice. You said your piece once, gently, and got waved off. Then one Tuesday the phone rings and it has all gone exactly the way you said it would. Here is the moment. The most satisfying sentence in the English language is sitting loaded on your tongue, fully earned, completely true, already assembled. I told you so. You could say it kindly. You could say it with nothing but a look. Every syllable has been paid for. And love picks up that perfect, deserved sentence, and sets it back down. Makes the tea instead. Asks what happens next. Helps load the truck.

Nobody hands out trophies for that, and the invisibility is the point. Half of Paul's portrait is refusals, and every one of them means walking away from a payoff that was genuinely available. The flash of being envied. The credit. The delicious last word. The little lift of watching your prediction come true. Those wins feel wonderful for about an hour. Love keeps declining them, not because it is weak but because it is busy protecting something better, the person in front of you, who needs a friend right now far more than a verdict. You were right. You said nothing. That silence was love doing its strongest work, in the one place only God could hear it.

🌍 Today's World, Ancient Truth
The Ledger Was Costing the Bookkeeper

Researchers recently ran the largest forgiveness experiment ever attempted, and they ran it in places with long memories. In a trial published in BMJ Public Health in 2024, a team led by psychologist Man Yee Ho, alongside forgiveness researcher Everett Worthington and Harvard's Tyler VanderWeele, gave 4,598 people at six sites in five countries, Colombia, South Africa, Ukraine, Indonesia, and Hong Kong, a plain do-it-yourself workbook. No therapist, no retreat, about three hours at a kitchen table. The pages walk one real hurt, a specific one with a name and a date, through five steps. Recall it honestly. Work at seeing the offender as human. Remember a time you were the one forgiven. Commit to canceling the debt. Hold on when the books try to reopen. Two weeks later, compared with people still waiting for their copy, the forgivers were measurably better. Less unforgiveness. Less depression. Less anxiety. And greater flourishing, the researchers' word for a life going well as a whole.

Notice who the trial was measuring. Not the people who did the wrong. The people holding the record of it. Paul's bookkeeping word pictured a wrong entered in the account so it could be collected later, and the modern data shows what that entry actually collects. It charges the bookkeeper. Every grudge on file was quietly billing its keeper in depression, in anxiety, in the unforgiveness itself, interest accruing for years on debts nobody was ever going to pay. Tearing up the page turned out to be the move that stopped the bleeding, exactly as the chapter said, and exactly as the cross showed. God did not wait to collect what we owed. He canceled the record that stood against us and nailed it to His cross, and He has been inviting His people into that accounting ever since. Love keeps no record of wrongs. That is not naive bookkeeping. It is the only bookkeeping that leaves the bookkeeper alive.

✝️ The Christ Connection

Watch the eight refusals hold at the one place they should have shattered. At the cross, Jesus had grounds for every behavior love declines. He could have envied, the Lord of glory watching soldiers gamble for His only robe. He could have boasted, one word summoning legions of angels to end the whole indignity. He was mocked and stayed silent, struck and did not strike back, provoked past any limit a human being has ever faced and never once became what they were. And the ledger. He was accumulating the greatest record of wrongs in history, hour by hour, nail by nail, and He looked at the men writing the entries and said Father, forgive them. The refusals of 1 Corinthians 13 are not weakness dressed up as virtue. They are the strongest will in the universe, declining every exit, staying on the cross out of love for the people who put Him there. Including us.

✅ Personal Application Challenge
You know whose file you keep. The person whose old offenses you can still recite by date. Today, retire one entry. Write the oldest wrong on a slip of paper, pray for that person by name while you hold it, then tear it up and throw it away. You are not saying it never happened. You are saying it will no longer collect interest. One entry, gone for good, today.
🙏 Closing Prayer

Father, today's list found us. We know the taste of every behavior on it, the envy that spikes at someone else's good news, the boast we disguise as an update, the sharp word that felt so right leaving our mouth, and the records. Lord, You know about our records. Some of us have kept certain books for years, accurate to the day. Forgive us for running love like a counting house. Teach us Your accounting instead, the kind that canceled a debt we could never have paid and then burned the ledger at Calvary. Pry the stylus out of our hands. Where we have been touchy, settle us. Where we have been self-seeking, turn our eyes to the person beside us. And when the old files call our name this week, remind us that we tore them up on purpose, in Your presence, because loved people learn to love. In Jesus' name, Amen.
